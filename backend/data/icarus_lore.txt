Icarus was given wings of feather and wax by his father Daedalus, so they could escape the labyrinth of Crete together.

Daedalus warned Icarus: fly neither too low, where sea spray would dampen the feathers, nor too high, where the sun would melt the wax.

Icarus, intoxicated by the freedom of flight, forgot his father's warning and climbed higher and higher toward the sun.

The wax in his wings melted as he neared the sun. The feathers came loose and fell away, one by one, into the sea below.

Icarus fell from the sky into the waters that would later bear his name, the Icarian Sea.

The myth is often read as a warning against hubris, yet it can equally be read as a story about the price of ambition, and whether the ascent was worth the fall.

Daedalus, grieving, continued to fly on alone, unable to save his son, carrying both the invention that gave them freedom and the grief of having built the wings that failed.

Some retellings emphasize not the fall but the flight itself: that for a brief moment, Icarus knew what it was to be free of the earth entirely.

The story endures because it holds two truths at once: reckless overreach, and the very human refusal to stay grounded when the sky is possible.
